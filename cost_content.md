<div class="answer-box"><span>Quick answer</span><p>Compare the net cost for the full degree, not one year of advertised tuition. Include compulsory fees, housing, food, transport, insurance, equipment, visa costs and realistic funding, then add a contingency for price and currency changes.</p></div><div class="editor-note"><strong>Editorial note:</strong> Education, admissions, fees and immigration rules can change. Check the linked official source again before applying or paying.</div><section><h2>Use one comparison period</h2><p>A three-year degree and a four-year degree cannot be compared using annual tuition alone. Create both an annual budget and a full-programme budget. Use the institution’s published programme length and mark costs that are estimates rather than confirmed charges.</p><p>Convert international options into one currency using the same exchange-rate date. Keep the original currency beside each figure, because exchange rates can move before fees are due.</p></section><section><h2>Record every university-billed charge</h2><p>Ask whether fees normally rise for continuing students and whether the institution provides a maximum or only a current estimate. Save the official fee page and the date accessed.</p><ul><li>Published tuition for the exact programme and study load</li><li>Registration, student-service, laboratory, technology and examination fees</li><li>Mandatory health insurance or health-service charges</li><li>Residence deposits, meal plans and campus housing charges</li><li>Programme-specific equipment, studio, field trip or placement costs</li></ul></section><section><h2>Estimate living costs from a realistic scenario</h2><p>Build a month-by-month estimate for rent, utilities, food, local transport, phone, clothing and personal expenses. Use the city and accommodation type you would actually choose. A national average is less useful than an estimate based on the campus neighbourhood and your likely contract length.</p><p>Include initial costs such as a housing deposit, bedding, kitchen items and local transport setup. International students should add flights, visa fees, health checks and document costs where relevant, using the official government source for the current requirement.</p></section><section><h2>Separate confirmed aid from possible aid</h2><p>Subtract grants and scholarships only when they are guaranteed or already awarded. Keep competitive scholarships in a separate scenario. Record renewal conditions, minimum grades, full-time study requirements and whether the award changes when tuition rises.</p><p>For U.S. comparisons, College Scorecard can provide context on average cost, financial aid and debt, but your individual aid offer is the controlling document. Use the same caution with any aggregated source.</p></section><section><h2>Run three budget scenarios</h2><p>The stress test shows whether the plan still works if one assumption changes. Do not rely on work income that your visa does not permit or that is not guaranteed.</p><ul><li>Expected: the most realistic tuition, housing and funding assumptions</li><li>Lower-cost: confirmed savings such as a cheaper housing option</li><li>Stress test: higher rent, fewer scholarships or an adverse currency move</li></ul></section><section><h2>Questions to send the finance office</h2><ul><li>Which charges are compulsory for this programme?</li><li>Can fees change after enrolment, and how are increases communicated?</li><li>What is the deposit and refund schedule?</li><li>Which scholarships are automatic, competitive or renewable?</li><li>Are placement, fieldwork, equipment or graduation costs excluded from tuition?</li></ul></section><section class="faq-section"><h2>Frequently asked questions</h2><details><summary>What is the difference between tuition and cost of attendance?</summary><p>Tuition is the charge for instruction. Cost of attendance is a broader estimate that can include fees, housing, food, books, transport and personal expenses.</p></details><details><summary>Should a scholarship be deducted before it is awarded?</summary><p>No. Put unconfirmed scholarships in a separate scenario so the core budget remains realistic.</p></details><details><summary>How much contingency should a student add?</summary><p>There is no universal percentage. Base it on the volatility of rent, exchange rates, travel and programme-specific costs, and keep emergency funds separate.</p></details></section><section class="source-section"><h2>Primary sources</h2><p>Sources were accessed for this guide on 20 August 2026.</p><ul><li><a href="https://collegescorecard.ed.gov/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">College Scorecard cost and aid data</a></li><li><a href="https://www.ucas.com/applying/after-you-apply/clearing-and-results-day/after-results-day/preparing-for-your-studies" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">UCAS preparing for study and budgeting</a></li><li><a href="https://w05.educanada.ca/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">EduCanada programme and cost search</a></li></ul></section>
