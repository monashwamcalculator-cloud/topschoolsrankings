<div class="answer-box"><span>Quick answer</span><p>Verify a university in the official government or regulator database for the country, then confirm the exact programme, campus, study mode and award with the institution. A ranking, social profile or agent brochure is not proof of accreditation.</p></div><div class="editor-note"><strong>Editorial note:</strong> Education, admissions, fees and immigration rules can change. Check the linked official source again before applying or paying.</div><section><h2>Why accreditation needs a separate check</h2><p>Accreditation and official recognition affect whether a qualification is accepted by employers, licensing bodies, other universities and—depending on the country—student-aid or visa systems. A university may be legitimate while a particular programme requires additional professional approval, so the institution and the course should be checked separately.</p><p>Do not treat a polished website, a high search position, a ranking badge or the word “international” as evidence. Start with an official database, record what you find and ask the institution to explain any mismatch in writing before you pay a deposit.</p></section><section><h2>United States: use the Department of Education database</h2><p>The U.S. Department of Education provides the Database of Accredited Postsecondary Institutions and Programs. Search the institution’s legal name and confirm the accrediting agency shown in the record. College Scorecard can then help you compare fields of study, typical costs, completion, debt and earnings data where available.</p><p>Accreditation is not the same as a guarantee of admission, credit transfer, professional licensure or a particular employment outcome. Ask the university and the receiving or licensing body about those questions directly.</p></section><section><h2>United Kingdom: check the provider and the course</h2><p>Use the official course and provider information available through UCAS and Discover Uni. Courses with similar names can differ in modules, assessment, placement options and outcomes, so compare the course page—not only the university brand.</p><p>For regulated careers, check the relevant professional body’s approved-programme list. If you will need a Student visa, also verify the current sponsor and visa requirements through GOV.UK before relying on an offer.</p></section><section><h2>Canada and Australia: verify international-study eligibility</h2><p>For Canada, international applicants should confirm that the school appears on the current Designated Learning Institution list and should check the exact campus and programme details. EduCanada advises applicants to confirm current programmes, tuition and deadlines with the institution because information changes.</p><p>For Australia, search CRICOS—the Australian Government register of providers and courses offered to students on student visas. Match the provider, course code, location and duration. QILT and ComparED can add student-experience and graduate-outcome context, but they do not replace CRICOS verification.</p></section><section><h2>A seven-step verification record</h2><ul><li>Search the official regulator or government database using the legal institution name.</li><li>Match the campus, programme title, qualification level and delivery mode.</li><li>Open the institution’s own course page and note the date you checked it.</li><li>Confirm tuition, mandatory fees, deposits and refund terms in writing.</li><li>Check any professional accreditation needed for your intended career.</li><li>Verify visa eligibility separately on the relevant government website.</li><li>Save screenshots, emails and links with your application records.</li></ul></section><section><h2>Red flags that justify pausing</h2><p>Pause if the name in the official database differs from the name on your offer, the agent refuses to provide a programme code, the institution pressures you to pay immediately, or employment and immigration outcomes are promised as guaranteed. Legitimate providers should be able to explain their status and direct you to an official record.</p></section><section class="faq-section"><h2>Frequently asked questions</h2><details><summary>Is a university ranking proof of accreditation?</summary><p>No. Rankings are editorial or analytical comparisons. Accreditation or official recognition must be checked with the relevant regulator or government database.</p></details><details><summary>Should I verify the programme as well as the university?</summary><p>Yes. Professional courses, branch campuses and different study modes can have separate approvals or recognition implications.</p></details><details><summary>What if the official record and offer letter do not match?</summary><p>Do not pay until the institution explains the difference in writing and the relevant authority confirms the correct status.</p></details></section><section class="source-section"><h2>Primary sources</h2><p>Sources were accessed for this guide on 20 August 2026.</p><ul><li><a href="https://ope.ed.gov/dapip/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">U.S. Department of Education accreditation database</a></li><li><a href="https://collegescorecard.ed.gov/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">U.S. College Scorecard</a></li><li><a href="https://discoveruni.gov.uk/course-comparison/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Discover Uni course comparison</a></li><li><a href="https://www.educanada.ca/study-plan-etudes/before-avant/apply-school_canada_demande-ecole.aspx?lang=eng" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">EduCanada application guidance</a></li><li><a href="https://cricos.education.gov.au/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Australian Government CRICOS</a></li></ul></section>
