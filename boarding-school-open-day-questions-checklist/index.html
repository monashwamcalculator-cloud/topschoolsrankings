Boarding School Open Day Questions: Parent Checklist

A practical checklist for comparing safeguarding, boarding life, academic support, fees, communication and student wellbeing at school visits.

Tables, images and URL checked 13 November 2026 · Verify time-sensitive facts
Quick answer

Prepare questions that uncover the reality of boarding life beyond the marketing brochure. Ask about weekend schedules, mobile phone policies, mental health support, bullying protocols, and exactly who supervises the dormitories at night. The best questions require specific examples rather than generic yes/no answers.

Editorial note: School policies and staffing structures change frequently. Always confirm critical policies regarding student welfare in writing with the admissions director before enrolling.

The Purpose of an Open Day

An open day or campus tour is a heavily curated marketing event designed to show the boarding school in its best possible light. The student tour guides are hand-picked, the dorms are meticulously cleaned, and the faculty are prepared for standard questions.

Your goal is to politely but firmly probe beneath this polished surface. The questions you ask should focus on the actual lived experience of a 14-year-old student living away from home for the first time. Focus heavily on pastoral care, weekend downtime, and disciplinary realities.

How to Use This Checklist on the Day

You will not get through sixty questions in one visit, and you are not meant to. Before you leave home, read the lists below and mark the ten questions that matter most for your child — the anxious child needs the pastoral answers, the sporty child needs the weekend answers, and every family needs the money answers. Split the work between the adults going: one person asks the questions, the other watches the child. Your child's face during the tour — bored, curious, quietly relieved — will tell you things no prospectus can. Give your child a job as well: three things they liked, three things they did not, and one thing that surprised them, written down before you reach home. Finally, take photographs of the ordinary places, not the impressive ones: a dormitory corridor, the dining hall queue, a noticeboard. Open days are staged; the ordinary corners are where the truth leaks out.

Questions About Pastoral Care and Wellbeing

Academic excellence means nothing if a child is miserable or unsupported. These questions help determine the school's true safety net.

  • Who exactly is on duty in the boarding house at 2:00 AM if a student is sick or having a panic attack? Are they teaching staff or dedicated houseparents?
  • How does the school handle homesickness during the difficult first three weeks of term? Can you give a specific example of the protocol?
  • What is the exact ratio of full-time school counsellors to boarding students? Are these counsellors available after standard school hours?
  • How is bullying, particularly cyberbullying, actively monitored and handled within the boarding houses?
  • What is the strict policy on mobile phones and laptops in bedrooms after lights out? How is this enforced?

Questions About the Boarding House Itself

The tour will show you a show dormitory. Ask about the real ones, in the houses your child would actually live in.

  • How many children share a dormitory in each year group, and does that number shrink as they get older?
  • Where do the houseparents live relative to the dormitories, and who covers the house when they are off duty?
  • How are roommates chosen in the first term, and what happens if a pairing clearly is not working?
  • What are the bathroom and shower arrangements, and are they ever queues at peak times?
  • Who does the laundry, how often, and what happens to the inevitable lost items?
  • Can students personalise their space — photos, bedding, a small lamp — or is everything standard issue?
  • Where do boarders keep valuables and passports, and who holds them for younger children?

Questions About Food, Health and Feeling Unwell

Children forgive a school almost anything except being hungry, or feeling ill with nobody noticing. Ask these directly.

  • Can we see a real week's menu — not the open day menu — including what is served at breakfast and in the evening?
  • How are food allergies and medical diets handled in the dining hall day to day?
  • What happens, step by step, when a boarder wakes up ill at 3:00 AM? Who decides between the medical centre, rest in house, or hospital?
  • Is there a nurse or medical centre on site, what are its hours, and which hospital does the school use?
  • Who gives prescribed medication, and where is it stored?
  • How quickly would parents be told if their child visited the medical centre?

Questions About Safeguarding and Safety

Every school will say safeguarding is its top priority. The useful answers are the specific ones.

  • Who is the designated safeguarding lead, and how would a student raise a concern about another student — or about a member of staff?
  • What is the visitor and sign-in policy for the boarding houses, including for parents?
  • What are the rules for leaving campus — exeats, town leave, and signing out — at your child's age?
  • How does the school handle phones, messaging apps and social media problems that start off campus but land in the dormitory?
  • When were the boarding houses last inspected, and can we read the inspection findings on boarding and welfare, not just academics?

Questions About Weekends and Downtime

Many boarding schools look incredible on a Tuesday afternoon but feel like ghost towns on a Sunday morning if a large percentage of students go home for the weekend.

  • What percentage of boarders actually stay on campus during a typical non-exeat (standard) weekend?
  • Are weekend activities mandatory or optional? Who organizes them?
  • If a student does not want to play competitive sports on a Saturday, what exact alternative activities are available to them?
  • How often are students allowed to leave the campus to visit the local town, and what is the supervision policy for these trips?

Questions About Academics and Prep (Homework)

Boarding schools must balance rigorous academics with the need for downtime. Understand how evening study works.

  • How is "prep" (homework) supervised in the evenings? Is it done in individual bedrooms or a communal study hall?
  • If a student is struggling with a specific subject like Maths, what structural support is available in the evenings? Do academic tutors visit the boarding houses?
  • How does the school communicate minor academic or behavioral issues to parents who live in a different country or time zone?

Questions to Ask the Student Tour Guide (Privately)

Student guides are usually honest if asked specific, relatable questions away from faculty ears.

  • What is the absolute best and worst thing about the food here? What do you eat on a Sunday evening?
  • If you have a problem with a teacher or another student, who is the first person you actually go to for help?
  • What happens if you break a minor rule, like being late for roll call?
  • What do most boarders actually do on a Sunday afternoon here?

What to Watch for Instead of Asking

Some of the most reliable information on an open day is never said out loud. Watch for these.

  • How students speak to the staff showing you around — and whether staff know individual students' names in the corridor.
  • The state of the dormitory you were not meant to see, glimpsed from a corridor: lived-in and relaxed, or suspiciously perfect?
  • Noticeboards: are they full of recent, varied activities, or the same faded posters?
  • Whether your student guide answers freely when a teacher steps away — and whether guides were clearly briefed on what not to mention.
  • The dining hall at a real mealtime if your visit overlaps one: noise, queues, and whether staff eat with students.
  • How the admissions team reacts to a difficult question. A school that answers a hard question calmly on an open day will usually answer it calmly in a crisis.

Money Questions Most Parents Forget to Ask

The fee on the website is the starting point, not the bill. Open day is the polite moment to ask what else families actually pay for — nobody will be offended, and every school expects it.

  • What is not included in the boarding fee? Ask specifically about trips, expeditions, uniform, sports kit, a laptop or tablet, exam fees and music or coaching extras.
  • How much have fees risen over the last three years, year by year?
  • What deposit is payable on acceptance, and in what circumstances is it refunded or kept?
  • If our circumstances change, what are the notice terms for leaving, and what happens to fees already paid?
  • Are bursaries reviewed every year, and can an award be reduced or removed — under what conditions?
  • For international families: what do airport transfers and holiday-period arrangements cost, and is guardianship arranged by the school or entirely by us?

When you compare schools, compare the full yearly figure you build from these answers, not the headline fee — our guide to boarding in the USA vs the UK shows how differently two systems bundle these costs.

Questions for International and Long-Distance Parents

If you will be in another time zone while your child boards, a second set of questions matters as much as the first.

  • Does the school require a locally based guardian, what exactly must the guardian be able to do, and does the school check them?
  • What happens at exeat weekends and half terms if our child cannot come home — who stays open, and at what cost?
  • How do parents' meetings and reports work across time zones? Are video meetings with houseparents routine?
  • How often will the houseparent contact us in the first term, and through which channel?
  • Are airport transfers organised by the school, and who accompanies younger boarders to check-in?
  • Where do belongings stay over the summer holidays, and can trunks be stored at school?

If you are still deciding between countries rather than between schools, work through the top UK boarding schools list alongside this checklist — the same questions expose very different answers across schools.

Creating Your Decision Matrix

Do not rely on memory. Immediately after leaving the campus, sit in the car and write down your impressions while they are fresh. Score the school on safety, warmth of the houseparents, honesty of the student guides, and the quality of the facilities. If a school gives evasive or defensive answers to your wellbeing questions, it is a massive red flag that should override any academic ranking.

After the Visit: The Same-Evening Debrief

Do this the same evening, for every school, in the same format — it is what turns three pleasant tours into one clear decision.

  1. Score the school separately on safety, warmth, honesty and facilities while your impressions are fresh — the existing decision matrix above works; just do it tonight, not next week.
  2. Write down your child's three likes, three dislikes and one surprise, in their words.
  3. List every question that was dodged, deferred or answered with a brochure. A short list is normal; a long one is data.
  4. Email the unanswered questions to admissions within two days. The speed and candour of the replies is itself a comparison point.
  5. Ask about a taster night or a normal school-day visit for your child. Schools confident in their boarding usually offer one — and a Tuesday in February tells a different story from an open day in May.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should the prospective student attend the open day?

Yes, absolutely. The child is the one who will be living there. Their intuitive feeling about the campus vibe and the friendliness of the current students is often the most accurate indicator of whether they will thrive.

Is it appropriate to ask about disciplinary statistics?

Yes. You are fully within your rights to ask how many students were suspended or expelled in the previous academic year and for what primary reasons (e.g., vaping, bullying, academic dishonesty). Transparency here is a sign of a healthy school culture.

What if we cannot visit in person because we live overseas?

Request a live 1-on-1 virtual tour where a student walks around with an iPad or phone. Crucially, ask for a private Zoom meeting with the specific Housemaster or Housemistress who will be responsible for your child's dorm, not just the admissions director.

What should we ask about phones and screen time?

Ask for the written policy, not the open-day summary: where phones sleep at night, whether bedrooms are device-free after lights out, how the rule is actually enforced, and what happens after a first and second breach. Then ask your student guide the same question privately, away from staff — the gap between the two answers tells you how the policy really works in the house.

When should we ask about fees and extra costs?

On the day itself, without embarrassment — admissions teams expect these questions, and the answers change the comparison more than almost anything else. Ask what the fee excludes (trips, uniform, devices, exam fees, airport transfers), how much fees rose in each of the last three years, and the deposit and notice terms. Build each school's true annual total from those answers before you compare schools on price.
