Best Universities in Europe for International Students: Top 100 Rankings, English-Taught Degrees & Tuition (2026)

The best universities in Europe for international students: the top 100 ranked, with English-taught degrees, non-EU tuition by country and post-study visas.

Tables, images and URL checked 18 November 2026 · Verify time-sensitive facts

Europe is where a strong degree and a manageable bill can still go together. The QS World University Rankings: Europe 2026 puts Oxford first, ETH Zurich second, Imperial College London and UCL tied third, and Cambridge fifth — and behind those five, this guide ranks 100 universities across the continent, from the UK and Ireland to Germany, France, the Netherlands, Switzerland and the Nordics.

This guide is written for international students. It covers the ranking itself, where English-taught degrees actually exist, what non-EU students pay in each country, and how long graduates can stay to look for work. The order below is our own editorial ranking, built with the weights in the methodology section; QS Europe 2026 and Times Higher Education's Europe table are the outside checks we measured it against. Tuition figures are projected ranges for non-EU students — confirm the exact fee on the university's own fees page before you apply, because fees change every year.

Best Universities in Europe at a Glance

  • Low-cost strongholds: Public universities in most German states still charge no tuition — only a small semester contribution. Baden-Württemberg is the exception at €1,500 a semester for non-EU students, and TUM charges €2,000 to €6,000 a semester depending on the programme.
  • The Swiss change: ETH Zurich and EPFL tripled fees for most international students from autumn 2025 — CHF 2,190 a semester (CHF 4,380 a year). Even so, that is far below leading UK fees.
  • UK fees are the outlier: Oxford's overseas undergraduate fees for 2026–27 run £37,380 to £62,820 a year, depending on the course. Continental Europe mostly sits between €0 and about €21,000 a year.
  • English where it counts: Master's degrees across Western Europe are mostly taught in English. Bachelor's degrees are a different story — the Netherlands, Ireland and the Nordics offer the widest choice in English; Germany, France and Switzerland keep most bachelor's teaching in the local language.
  • Staying on after graduation: Germany gives graduates up to 18 months to look for work; the Netherlands gives 12 months with no work-permit requirement; Ireland gives 12 months after a bachelor's and up to 24 after a master's; Finland gives up to two years.

A Closer Look: Top 5 Universities in Europe (2026 Overview)

These five lead both our table and the QS Europe 2026 ranking — Oxford first, ETH Zurich second, Imperial and UCL tied third, Cambridge fifth. Each one is different in what it offers an international student, so read them for fit, not just for rank.

1. University of Oxford — Oxford, England

Oxford tops the QS Europe 2026 ranking with a perfect score of 100. It is a federation of 36 colleges, three societies and four permanent private halls — 43 colleges and halls in all — and its Bodleian Library opened in 1602. Teaching is built around small-group tutorials in the colleges. Overseas undergraduate fees for 2026–27 run £37,380 to £62,820 a year depending on the course, with clinical medicine higher. University of Oxford profile

2. University of Cambridge — Cambridge, England

Cambridge sits fifth in QS Europe 2026 and second in our table. Its 31 self-governing colleges run the university's famous supervisions — regular small-group teaching with a college academic — across six Schools covering the arts, sciences, medicine and technology. The Fitzwilliam Museum, founded in 1816, holds more than half a million artworks and antiquities. University of Cambridge profile

3. Imperial College London — London, England

Imperial is tied third in QS Europe 2026. Chartered in 1907, it teaches only science, engineering, medicine and business — no humanities departments at all — from its South Kensington campus, with a second major campus at White City. That narrow focus is the point: every department sits inside those four fields. Imperial College London profile

4. ETH Zurich — Zurich, Switzerland

ETH Zurich is second in QS Europe 2026 and the highest-ranked university in continental Europe. It is Switzerland's federal institute of technology, and its strength is engineering and the natural sciences. Since autumn 2025 most international students pay CHF 2,190 a semester (CHF 4,380 a year) — three times the old fee, and still far below UK levels.

5. University College London (UCL) — London, England

UCL is tied third in QS Europe 2026. Founded in 1826, it was the first university in England to admit students regardless of religion, and the first to admit women, in 1878. Its main campus sits on Gower Street in central London, and it teaches across eleven faculties — the broadest range of any university in this top five. University College London profile

English-Taught Degrees in Europe: Where to Look

The first question is not which university, but which language your degree is taught in — and the honest answer depends on the level. At master's level, English is the norm across Western Europe: Germany, France, Switzerland and the Netherlands all run large English-taught master's catalogues. At bachelor's level the choice narrows sharply. The Netherlands, Ireland and the Nordic countries offer the widest range of English-taught bachelor's degrees. In Germany, France and Switzerland, most bachelor's programmes still teach in the local language, with English options concentrated in a smaller set of programmes.

Check before you shortlist. Germany's DAAD international programmes database lists English-taught degrees programme by programme, and the Study in NL register does the same for the Netherlands — both are linked in the Sources section below. Two things to verify on the programme page itself: that the whole degree is taught in English (some bachelor's programmes switch to the local language in later years), and what English test score the programme asks for — most ask for IELTS 6.5 to 7.0 or TOEFL iBT 90 to 100, with higher bands for competitive faculties.

Living costs are separate and often decide the budget: Germany asks visa applicants to show €11,904 for a year in a blocked account (see the Sperrkonto section below), and Zurich and Lausanne run roughly CHF 1,800 to CHF 2,500 a month. Always budget tuition and living costs together — a €0-tuition degree in an expensive city can cost more than a mid-fee degree in a cheaper one.

Post-Study Visas: How Long You Can Stay After Graduating

A degree is only half the decision. The other half is how long the country lets you stay and look for work once you graduate — and the answer ranges from 12 months to two years.

1. Germany: Technical Focus & 18-Month Jobseeker Status

Germany remains the strongest option for engineering and science graduates who want to stay. Apart from TUM's fees and Baden-Württemberg's €1,500-a-semester charge, public universities charge no tuition. Graduates can claim an 18-month residence permit to look for qualified work, and may take any job — even outside their field — while they search. A qualifying job then leads to a skilled-worker permit or an EU Blue Card.

2. The Netherlands: Multilingual Integration & Orientation Year

With the widest choice of English-taught bachelor's degrees in Europe, the Netherlands pairs that with the simplest post-study route: the zoekjaar, or orientation year. It is a 12-month permit for graduates of Dutch institutions (apply within three years of graduating) that gives free access to the Dutch labour market — no work permit, no sponsor and no salary threshold during the year. After it, the Highly Skilled Migrant permit applies at a reduced salary threshold.

3. Switzerland: Elite Training Against Living Costs

Both ETH Zurich and EPFL provide advanced computational and physical laboratories at tuition that is still modest by UK standards — CHF 2,190 a semester for most international students since autumn 2025. The real cost is living: budget roughly CHF 1,800 to CHF 2,500 a month in Zurich and Lausanne. Switzerland is not in the EU, so its post-study permit is a separate Swiss route — check the current rules with the university's international office before planning around staying on.

4. Ireland: 12 Months After a Bachelor's, Up to 24 After a Master's

Ireland's Third Level Graduate Programme (Stamp 1G) is the most generous route in this guide for master's graduates: 12 months after an honours bachelor's degree, and up to 24 months — granted as two 12-month blocks — after a master's or PhD. During that time graduates can work full-time, 40 hours a week, with no employment permit. You must apply within six months of your results and hold a valid student permission (Stamp 2) when you apply.

5. Nordic Region: Specialized Research & Career Visas

Sweden, Denmark and Finland all charge tuition to non-EU students, but they compensate with long post-study windows. Sweden grants up to 12 months to look for work after a bachelor's or master's (up to 18 months for doctoral graduates). Finland goes further: up to two years to look for work or start a business. Both require proof that you can support yourself for the permit period, so budget for it before you apply.

Applying as a Non-EU Student: Deadlines, Funds and Work Rules

Applications from outside the EU run on earlier deadlines than domestic ones, because visa processing and document checks take months. Most universities open applications between November and December and close international applications between mid-January and April for autumn entry — but the exact dates are set by each university, so treat the programme page as the only deadline that counts.

ECTS Framework and Global Equivalence

Higher education across the Continent is standardized through the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS). Academic programs calibrate workloads objectively: standard bachelor's curriculums encompass 180 to 240 credits over three or four years, whereas master's frameworks require 60 to 120 credits. International credentials organizations (including WES) routinely endorse these units as completely equivalent to four-year North American undergraduate degrees or two-year graduate programs.

Financial Escrow Requirements (Sperrkonto)

Immigration processing demands concrete proof of subsistence funding. In Germany, this requires prospective students to deposit mandatory annual allowances into a dedicated Blocked Account (currently calibrated at roughly €11,904). Corresponding legal guarantees or direct bank deposits apply when lodging entry permits in France, Sweden, and the Netherlands.

Working While You Study

Two countries, two sets of rules. In Germany, non-EU students may work up to 140 full days or 280 half days a year; jobs as student assistants at the university do not count against that limit. In the Netherlands, non-EU students may work up to 16 hours a week during term, or full-time in June, July and August — and the employer, not the student, must hold the work permit (TWV). In both countries, breaking the work rules can put your residence permit at risk, so confirm the conditions printed on your own permit before you start a job.

Local Language Importance for Internships

Even if your formal coursework is delivered entirely in English, navigating local corporate environments, securing off-campus industrial working-student contracts, and managing regional administration necessitates achieving practical conversational proficiency (CEFR B1/B2 level) before completing your studies.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Which countries in Continental Europe charge no tuition fees to international students?

Germany is the clearest case: public universities in most German states charge no tuition at all — only a small semester contribution — though Baden-Württemberg charges non-EU students €1,500 a semester and TUM charges €2,000 to €6,000 a semester. In Czechia, programmes taught in Czech are tuition-free at public universities, including for international students. In Austria, non-EU students pay a set fee of about €1,500 a year at public universities. English-taught degrees in all three countries carry fees of their own, so the language of instruction decides the price as much as the country does.

What caused tuition updates at German universities like TUM?

Federal state policies determine academic fees in Germany. The regional legislature in Bavaria permitted institutions to enact selective non-EU fee schedules to improve educational infrastructure, leading the Technical University of Munich (TUM) to introduce fees between €2,000 and €6,000 per term. The state of Baden-Württemberg operates a separate statutory requirement charging non-EU applicants €1,500 per semester. Most other German states still charge no tuition at public universities.

What is the purpose of the Dutch Orientation Year (Zoekjaar)?

The Zoekjaar is a one-year residency permit granted by the Dutch Immigration and Naturalisation Service (IND) to international alumni of recognized Dutch institutions. The holder can work without restrictive employer authorizations or minimum salary caps, providing sufficient runway to transition into an employment contract under the Highly Skilled Migrant scheme.

Are bachelor's degrees across Europe widely taught in English?

Availability depends significantly on destination. The Netherlands, Ireland, and Nordic nations provide a wide array of accredited, English-conducted undergraduate degrees. Conversely, institutions in Germany, France, and Switzerland focus their primary English catalogs on master's and doctoral degrees, maintaining local language entrance requirements for the majority of bachelor's tracks.

How does tuition compare between Continental Europe and the UK?

Tuition across Continental Europe is far lower than in the UK. Leading UK universities charge overseas students roughly £20,000 to £60,000 or more a year — Oxford's overseas undergraduate fees for 2026–27 run £37,380 to £62,820 depending on the course. Most Continental universities charge between €0 and about €21,000 a year: Germany's public universities charge no tuition in most states, France charges non-EU students €2,902 a year for a bachelor's in 2026–27, and ETH Zurich charges most international students CHF 2,190 a semester (CHF 4,380 a year) since autumn 2025.
