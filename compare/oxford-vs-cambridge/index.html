Oxford vs Cambridge: UK University Comparison Guide (2026)

Oxford vs Cambridge compared on verified figures: 2025-entry offer rates (16.7% vs 21.7%), the 15 October UCAS deadline, UAT-UK tests and fees.

Tables, images and URL checked 13 November 2026 · Verify time-sensitive facts

Oxford or Cambridge is the first fork in the road for almost every top-end UK application — and it really is a fork, because you cannot apply to both. In Oxford's own words: "It is not possible to apply to both the University of Oxford and the University of Cambridge in the same UCAS cycle." For 2025 entry, Oxford received 23,329 applications and made 3,891 offers (an offer rate of 16.7 per cent, calculated from its published figures), while Cambridge received 22,513 applications and made 4,893 offers (21.7 per cent calculated; Cambridge's own published "success rate" — acceptances as a share of applications — is 16.3 per cent). Both are brutally competitive, but they are not the same competition: the courses are structured differently, the admissions tests have just been overhauled, the interview formats differ, and for international students the fee structures do not work the same way at all.

Everything below is drawn from the two universities' and UCAS's own published pages — admissions statistics, fee schedules and application guides — checked on 13 November 2026 and listed in full at the end. Where a percentage is our arithmetic on official counts, we say so. Start with the two profiles for the full picture of each university: University of Oxford and University of Cambridge.

A closer look at both universities

University of Oxford — Oxford, England

Oxford is the older of the two and, by total student numbers, slightly the larger university: 26,800 students in all, of whom 12,460 are undergraduates (figures as at 1 December 2025). Its 39 colleges — plus four permanent private halls — are spread through a compact city centre, so the university and the city are thoroughly mixed together. Teaching centres on the tutorial: Oxford describes students meeting their tutor weekly with one or two other students to discuss prepared work, on top of lectures and classes. Oxford's course list is built from single subjects and a large number of named joint schools — combinations such as Philosophy, Politics and Economics (PPE), or Physics and Philosophy — each with its own admissions requirements. The full route in, tests and deadlines included, is in our Oxford admissions guide.

University of Cambridge — Cambridge, England

Cambridge has a marginally larger undergraduate body — 12,850 undergraduates within 24,927 students in total — organised into 31 Colleges, 29 of which admit undergraduates. Its signature teaching format is the supervision: small-group teaching led by a supervisor, which Cambridge presents as the core of its undergraduate education alongside lectures and practicals. Cambridge groups subjects into Triposes, and some of its degrees are deliberately broad at entry — Natural Sciences, above all, lets you start across the sciences and specialise later. The city is smaller than Oxford and even more completely shaped by the university and its river, the Cam. There is no Cambridge admissions guide on this site yet; the Cambridge profile covers the key facts, and a full guide is on our list.

Applying: one choice, an October deadline, new tests

The single most important fact in this comparison is the mutual exclusion: one UCAS cycle, one of the two universities. Choose by course first — because the courses genuinely differ (see below) — and only then by city or reputation.

The deadline. For autumn 2027 entry, UCAS applications for both universities close on 15 October 2026 at 18:00 UK time — months earlier than most UK courses. Cambridge adds its own step: the My Cambridge Application, due by 22 October 2026. Miss the October deadline and there is no later route in for that cycle at either university.

The tests — newly overhauled. Oxford has retired its own long-running admissions tests (the MAT was sat for the last time in October 2025). From the 2026 application round, both universities use the same UAT-UK test family, taken in an October sitting (12–16 October 2026, right against the UCAS deadline): the ESAT (Oxford: Biomedical Sciences, Engineering, Physics; Cambridge: Engineering, Natural Sciences, Veterinary Medicine, Chemical Engineering), the TMUA (Oxford: Computer Science, Mathematics; Cambridge: Computer Science, Economics, Mathematics), and the TARA (Oxford only: PPE, Economics and Management, Human Sciences, Psychology). Law at both uses the LNAT and Medicine the UCAT. Two qualifications matter: many Oxford courses now require no admissions test at all, and Cambridge also runs its own college assessments for some subjects. STEP, the famous Cambridge mathematics papers, is not a pre-interview test — it is an offer condition for Cambridge Mathematics (typically grade 1 in papers II and III), taken after you apply.

The interviews. Oxford interviews are all online, in December 2026. Cambridge interviews run across a defined period, 7–18 December 2026, and may be online or in person depending on the College. Both universities describe interviews as academic conversations about your subject — closer to a tutorial or supervision than to a job interview.

Applying to Oxbridge in one cycle, step by step

  1. Choose your course, then your university — the exclusion rule means the course decision decides the university more often than the other way round.
  2. Register for the right admissions test over the summer (ESAT, TMUA or TARA via UAT-UK; LNAT for Law; UCAT for Medicine) and sit it in the 12–16 October window where applicable.
  3. Submit UCAS by 15 October 2026, 18:00 UK time — and, for Cambridge, complete the My Cambridge Application by 22 October.
  4. Prepare for a December interview in your subject: Oxford's is online; Cambridge's may be online or in person at your College (7–18 December).
  5. Hold any offer against its conditions — for Cambridge Mathematics that can include STEP grades in June — and firm your choice in UCAS.

Costs for international students: read the structure, not the headline

For 2027 entry, Oxford's overseas tuition runs from £39,620 to £66,580 a year depending on the course — and that is the whole university charge: Oxford does not add a separate college fee. Cambridge publishes five tuition groups for the same year — £30,798 at the bottom, £34,356 for Mathematics, then £40,296 and £46,872, up to £70,554 for Medicine and Veterinary Medicine — but Cambridge then charges a College fee of £12,500–£15,950 a year on top. Do the arithmetic and the two universities land much closer than the headlines suggest: a mid-band Cambridge course at £40,296 plus a mid-range college fee is roughly a £54,000 year, squarely inside Oxford's all-in range. One genuine Cambridge advantage: its tuition fees are fixed for the duration of your course, so the group you start on is the group you finish on. Living costs differ by city and college rather than by university brand — budget them from the course-and-college total, not from either university's cheapest band.

The courses are not the same courses

This is where the choice should actually be made, because the structures differ in ways that affect three or four years of study. Take the classic social-science comparison: Oxford's PPE starts all students on Philosophy, Politics and Economics together in the first year, then lets you specialise in two of the three — or keep all three. Cambridge's closest counterpart, HSPS (Human, Social, and Political Sciences), is built from Politics and International Relations, Sociology and Social Anthropology, in single-subject or two-subject tracks — and it contains no economics at all; Cambridge Economics is a separate, mathematics-heavy degree with its own admissions test (the TMUA). In the sciences, the contrast is reversed: Cambridge's Natural Sciences is one broad degree — the most applied-to framework of its kind in this comparison (2,644 applications in the 2025 cycle, with a 21.8 per cent success rate) — in which you begin across several sciences and narrow down year by year. Oxford offers no equivalent: you apply to a specific science degree from day one. Oxford, in turn, offers a longer list of named joint degrees than Cambridge's Tripos combinations. Neither design is better; they suit different applicants. If you already know your subject precisely, Oxford's direct entry may fit. If you want a year to discover which science is yours, Cambridge's breadth is built for exactly that.

Teaching and daily life: tutorials, supervisions and two small cities

Both universities teach in very small groups, and both make the college — not the department — the centre of undergraduate daily life: you live, eat and socialise in your college, while lectures and laboratories are university-run. The formats have different names and slightly different shapes. Oxford's tutorials put you with your tutor and typically one or two other students, discussing work you have prepared, usually weekly for each paper. Cambridge's supervisions do the same job in small supervisor-led groups. In practice the two experiences are cousins — the honest difference is branding and rhythm, not quality. The cities differ more than the teaching. Oxford is the larger, busier city, with the university threaded through a working urban centre; Cambridge is smaller, quieter and dominated by its colleges and the river. With 39 colleges and halls at Oxford against 31 Colleges at Cambridge (29 undergraduate), college choice matters at both — size, location, accommodation guarantees and subject community vary far more between colleges than the universities' brands suggest, so research the college list as seriously as the course list. Both sit within commuting distance of London (Oxford roughly an hour by train, Cambridge a little under), which matters for internships and everything after graduation.

Choosing between them

Since the system forces one application, make the course do the choosing. Write down the exact degree you would study at each university and compare the actual papers: if it is PPE against HSPS, notice that one contains economics and the other does not; if it is science, decide whether you want Oxford's immediate specialisation or Cambridge's broad Natural Sciences runway. Then check your test: your course may need the ESAT, TMUA or TARA at one university and something else — or nothing — at the other, and the October sitting sits hard against the UCAS deadline, so the test calendar can quietly make the decision for you if you leave it late. Money rarely separates them once the Cambridge college fee is added to Cambridge's lower headline bands, except that Cambridge's fixed-for-duration tuition protects you against rises. City and college are the honest tiebreakers: a bigger, busier Oxford against a smaller, river-centred Cambridge, and 39 colleges against 31 — visit an open day at the one your course points to, and spend your visit in the colleges, not just the postcard streets.

Use a decision framework, not a winner label

For Oxbridge specifically, work through five questions in this order. One: which university's version of your course would you rather study for three years — read the papers, not the prospectus covers. Two: which admissions test does that version require, and does its October date fit your school calendar? Three: what is your full annual cost at each — tuition band plus, at Cambridge, the college fee. Four: which city's daily life suits you, and which three or four colleges at that university fit your subject and budget? Five — and only five: whose interview format plays to your strengths, online at Oxford or possibly in person at Cambridge? The brand names are identical in value; the answers to those five questions are not.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I apply to both Oxford and Cambridge in the same year?

No. Oxford states it directly: "It is not possible to apply to both the University of Oxford and the University of Cambridge in the same UCAS cycle." You must choose one — which is why comparing the courses, not just the universities, matters so much before the 15 October deadline.

Which is harder to get into, Oxford or Cambridge?

On overall 2025-entry figures, Oxford made offers to 16.7 per cent of its 23,329 applicants and Cambridge to 21.7 per cent of its 22,513 (both calculated from official counts; Cambridge's published success rate, counting acceptances, is 16.3 per cent). Course-level competition swamps that gap: Oxford's Economics and Management drew 18.8 applicants per place, while Cambridge Computer Science had a 7.4 per cent success rate and Natural Sciences 21.8 per cent. Your course, not the university average, sets your odds.

Do I need to take an admissions test?

It depends on the course. From the 2026 round both universities use the UAT-UK tests — ESAT, TMUA and (Oxford only) TARA — assigned by subject, with the LNAT for Law and the UCAT for Medicine, sat in an October window (12–16 October 2026). Oxford has retired its own tests and many of its courses now require no test; Cambridge adds college assessments for some subjects. STEP is not an entry test — it is an offer condition for Cambridge Mathematics, taken after offers.

How much does it cost as an international student?

For 2027 entry, Oxford's overseas tuition is £39,620–£66,580 per year with no separate college fee. Cambridge's tuition groups run £30,798–£70,554 per year, but a College fee of £12,500–£15,950 per year is charged on top; Cambridge tuition is fixed for the duration of your course. Once the college fee is added, the real annual costs at the two universities overlap heavily.

How do the interviews differ?

Oxford interviews all take place online in December 2026. Cambridge interviews run from 7 to 18 December 2026 and are online or in person depending on the College. Both are academic, subject-focused conversations modelled on the universities' own teaching — Oxford's tutorials and Cambridge's supervisions — rather than general interviews about your personal statement.
