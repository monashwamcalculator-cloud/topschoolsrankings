USA vs UK Boarding Schools: Fees, Admissions & Curriculum (2026)

USA vs UK boarding schools compared on verified figures: the ISC’s £43,095 average UK boarding fee (2025 census), entry routes (UKiset and Common Entrance vs the SSAT and common applications), GCSE/A-level vs the US diploma and AP, Child Student vs F-1 visas, and guardianship rules.

Tables, images and URL checked 13 November 2026 · Verify time-sensitive facts

"USA or UK?" is the first decision most international families make about boarding school — and it is really a decision between two systems, not two schools. The UK runs the larger, older, more standardised sector: 423 schools in the Independent Schools Council alone offer boarding, the Boarding Schools' Association counts more than 77,000 boarders in the UK of whom about 29,000 are international, and there is even an official average fee — £43,095 a year in the ISC's 2025 census, before VAT. The US sector is smaller, more decentralised and harder to summarise: its association, TABS, publishes no current average tuition at all, which is why every "average US boarding fee" you read online is somebody's estimate. The curricula differ just as sharply — GCSEs then specialising A levels in the UK, against a broad grades 9–12 diploma with Advanced Placement courses in the US — and so do the entry tests, the visas and the guardianship rules. This page compares the two systems only on figures and rules published by official bodies, each one sourced at the end.

Two habits will help you read it. First, costs: the UK figures here are the ISC's published census averages; for the US we deliberately print no average, because no official one exists — any school-by-school comparison must be built from the schools' own fee pages. Second, process: the UK has national fixtures (an official assessment framework, a defined visa category for schoolchildren) while the US runs on common platforms and school-set rules. For the visit itself, whichever country you choose, use our open day questions checklist — the questions that expose a weak boarding house work in both systems.

A closer look at both systems

United Kingdom — the boarding system

Boarding is woven into the UK's independent sector at national scale: 423 ISC schools offer it, and the ISC counted 25,526 non-British pupils with parents living overseas in its 2025 census, 93 per cent of them boarders. The academic spine is national and exam-led: GCSEs at 14–16 — described by Ofqual as the main school-leaving qualification — then two-year A levels, the main pre-university qualification, pitched at the same level as the IB Diploma. Entry for younger pupils runs through shared fixtures such as ISEB Common Entrance (sat at 11+ and 13+), and international applicants are commonly assessed through UKiset, a single test whose results can be sent to multiple schools. Around the academics sits a formal care framework: boarding schools operate as visa sponsors under the Child Student route, and the BSA runs a Certified Guardian scheme for the UK-based guardians international families appoint.

United States — the boarding system

US boarding is a private, school-by-school market rather than a national system. High school means grades 9–12 (the span the Department of Homeland Security uses for F-1 schooling), and the academic currency is the school's own diploma, strengthened by Advanced Placement: College Board describes AP as college-level coursework, with 40 courses and exams whose scores earn credit or placement at nearly all US colleges. Admissions run through shared platforms rather than shared exams alone — the SSAT test (verbal, quantitative and reading sections, taken across grades 3–11), the SAO common application that lets a family "submit once to any school that accepts it", and Gateway's common Candidate Profile. What the US system does not have is a national fee statistic: TABS, the boarding schools' association, publishes no current average tuition, so honest US budgeting is done school by school.

Admissions: shared fixtures vs shared platforms

The UK route. Schools set their own admissions, but the fixtures are widely shared. For prep-school leavers, ISEB Common Entrance is the common currency: at 13+ it is sat in Year 8 with English, mathematics and science compulsory (sessions in November, January and May–June), and there is an 11+ version sat in Year 6. International applicants usually start earlier in the chain with UKiset, the assessment many British independent schools ask overseas students to take — one test, shared with multiple schools — before school-specific interviews and papers. The US route. Most boarding schools ask for the SSAT, and applications run through common platforms: the SAO, the Enrollment Management Association's standard application online, or Gateway to Prep Schools, whose Candidate Profile is the primary common form for its participating schools. There is no national deadline in either country — each school sets its own — but the machinery differs: in the UK you are largely being measured against national benchmarks (UKiset, Common Entrance), while in the US you are assembling one strong file and reusing it across schools.

Applying across the two systems, step by step

  1. Decide the curriculum first — A-level depth from 16 or US breadth to 18 — because it decides the country more honestly than any school name does.
  2. Register for the right assessment: UKiset if you are applying to the UK from overseas; the SSAT for US schools that require it.
  3. Build one application file per system: SAO or Gateway for the US; school-by-school forms (often fed by your UKiset profile) for the UK.
  4. Visit or take virtual tours with the same question list in both countries — pastoral care, weekends, and who is on duty at night matter more than the prospectus in either system.
  5. Start the visa track as soon as a place is offered: a CAS from your UK school's sponsor licence for the Child Student route, or an I-20 from your US school's SEVP certification for the F-1.

Visas and guardians: the formal differences

UK. Boarding pupils aged 4–17 use the Child Student visa: the school must be a licensed sponsor, it issues a Confirmation of Acceptance for Studies (CAS), the published application fee is £558, and you must show funds covering course and boarding fees for one academic year; boarding at the school counts as the child's living arrangement. Around that sits guardianship: when a child is not in boarding accommodation — exeats, holidays, or living out — government guidance requires suitable care arrangements, which can include a nominated guardian for stays of up to 27 consecutive days, and the BSA's Certified Guardian Scheme exists to professionalise exactly that role (its standard: a guardian "represents the parent… doing what a caring parent would do if they were in the UK", with a lead guardian who is 25 or over and permanently resident). US. Boarding students use the F-1 visa: the school must be SEVP-certified and issues the Form I-20 that starts the process. The Department of Homeland Security draws the lines clearly — private K-12 schooling on an F-1 can run at any grade without the 12-month cap that applies to public high school. There is no federal guardianship scheme equivalent to the BSA's; living arrangements and local contacts are handled school by school, which is one more reason the US file needs closer reading than the UK one.

Student life: houses, weekends and the shape of the week

Both systems board children in houses or dormitories under resident staff, and both fill weekends with sport and activities — the lived differences are subtler than the structural ones. The UK school's rhythm is set by national exam seasons (GCSE and A-level summers dominate the calendar) and by the exeat-and-holiday pattern that makes the guardian question so practical there. The US school's rhythm is set by its own calendar of terms, breaks and interscholastic sport, and its broader curriculum keeps more subjects — and more timetable variety — alive until graduation. Fit questions beat system questions at this level: a child who knows their subjects early often thrives on A-level focus; a child still discovering strengths usually prefers the American breadth. Use a decision framework, not a winner label

Four questions settle most families' version of this choice. One: curriculum timing — is your child ready to specialise at 16 (the UK's A-level bet) or better served keeping every door open to 18 (the US diploma)? Two: where the degree is headed — A levels are the native currency of UK universities and the diploma-plus-AP file is the native currency of US colleges, though each is accepted across the Atlantic every year. Three: the care framework — the UK wraps international boarders in a formal sponsor-and-guardian structure you can inspect; the US leaves more to each school's own rules, so the reading load is higher. Four: money you can actually verify — the UK publishes a census average you can anchor to (£43,095 before VAT in 2025); the US publishes none, so the honest comparison is your shortlist's real fees, extras included, on both sides. Answer those four and the country usually chooses itself — then the real work, choosing the school, begins.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which is cheaper, boarding in the USA or the UK?

The UK is the only side with an official answer: the ISC's 2025 census puts the average boarding fee at £43,095 a year before VAT (£51,714 with VAT applied), across its 423 boarding schools. For the US, TABS publishes no average tuition, so any single "average US boarding fee" figure is an estimate, not a statistic. In practice both countries span a wide range — famous names in either sit far above their sector's middle — so compare the full cost of your actual shortlist, extras included, rather than a national average.

Which curriculum is better: A levels or the US diploma with AP?

They optimise for different students. The UK path specialises early: GCSEs (the main school-leaving qualification) at 14–16, then two-year A levels in a few subjects, at the same qualification level as the IB Diploma. The US path keeps breadth to 18 through the grades 9–12 diploma, with Advanced Placement offering 40 college-level courses whose scores earn credit or placement at nearly all US colleges. University admissions teams on both sides of the Atlantic read both files fluently; choose by how your child learns, not by a supposed hierarchy.

What tests do international students take for each country?

For the UK, overseas applicants are commonly assessed first through UKiset — the test many British independent schools ask international students to take, shareable across multiple schools — with ISEB Common Entrance (11+ and 13+) sitting underneath the prep-school route. For the US, most boarding schools ask for the SSAT (verbal, quantitative and reading), and applications are usually filed through a common platform — the SAO or Gateway — rather than school by school. Neither country runs a single national school deadline; each school sets its own.

Does my child need a guardian in each country?

In the UK, effectively yes — through structures you can verify: your child's school sponsors the Child Student visa, government guidance requires suitable care arrangements whenever the child is not in boarding accommodation (a nominated guardian can cover stays of up to 27 consecutive days), and the BSA runs a Certified Guardian Scheme whose guardians formally represent the parent in the UK. In the US there is no equivalent federal scheme; guardianship and local-contact arrangements are set by each school, so check them in writing before accepting a place.

Which student visa does a boarder need?

UK boarders aged 4–17 use the Child Student visa: a licensed sponsor school issues a CAS, the application fee is £558, and families must show funds for one academic year's course and boarding fees. US boarders use the F-1 visa: the school must be SEVP-certified and issues the Form I-20; private K-12 study on an F-1 carries no time cap (the 12-month limit applies to public high school). Start the visa track the moment a place is offered — both routes are document-heavy.
