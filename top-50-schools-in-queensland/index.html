Top 50 Schools in Queensland: 2025 ATAR Rankings

Queensland’s 50 highest-performing schools ranked by the median ATAR each school published for its Class of 2025 — with the state’s missing official league table, the consent-based figures and the set-aside schools all explained, plus parent FAQs.

Tables, images and URL checked 13 November 2026 · Verify time-sensitive facts

Queensland school rankings come with an honesty problem, and this page starts by naming it: there is no official Queensland league table. Since 2020 the state's authorities have published no school-by-school results file at all — unlike New South Wales and Victoria, where our Sydney and Victoria rankings rest on official workbooks. So this Top 50 is built the only way a Queensland ranking can be built: from the median ATAR each school itself published for its Class of 2025 (subject results released 17 December 2025, ATARs on 18 December; 57,909 students completed Year 12 statewide, 30,167 of them received an ATAR, and 37 students scored the maximum 99.95). On that measure, Queensland's strongest published median belongs to Brisbane Girls Grammar School — 96.45, and the state's number two is not in Brisbane at all: Whitsunday Anglican School in Mackay, at 96.00.

Read the table with its limits in plain view. Each median is calculated by the school itself, from the students who consented to share their ATAR with it, and schools count their cohorts differently — so two medians a point apart are not proof that one school outperformed another. What this ranking gives you is the best map available: every figure traced to the school's own publication or to the publisher harvests of those publications, disagreements resolved in favour of the school's own current figure and disclosed in the Sources note, and three schools set aside entirely because their published median and their published band shares cannot both be correct. The methodology note below explains all of it — in Queensland, the caveats are not fine print; they are part of the result.

How this ranking works — and what Queensland doesn't publish

The ATAR is a rank, not a score: calculated by QTAC from each student's best five scaled subject results, it runs from 0.00 to 99.95 against the whole Year 12 age cohort, so a 90+ ATAR sits roughly in the top tenth of the age group. Only about half of Queensland's Year 12 students receive one (52 per cent in 2025) — the rest take vocational and other pathways — and QTAC puts the statewide median ATAR of recipients at close to 80. A school's median ATAR is the middle result among its own ATAR students: half scored above it, half below. It is the Queensland figure most worth ranking on, because it describes the typical student rather than a handful of stars.

Now the limits, which are structural. Queensland authorities have released no per-school results since 2020, and a school can only see a student's ATAR if the student consents to share it — so every median here is self-calculated and self-published by the school, over a cohort the school defines: all ATAR-eligible students at one school, only consenting students at another (Brisbane Girls Grammar discloses that its figures rest on the 93.57 per cent who consented; St Margaret's, on 123 of its 128 eligible students), QCE students only at Churchie, QCE and IB combined at St Peters Lutheran College. Students who share their results may not be a random sample. Where a school's own current figure conflicts with a publisher's table, we print the school's — publishers do carry stale figures (one major table prints Matthew Flinders' 2022 median as its 2025 one). Rows marked † in the table rest on a single publisher's harvest of school-published figures (Better Education's extended list) with no own-site confirmation we could find: usable, but a step below the rest, and marked so you can weigh them yourself. One pattern deserves a sentence of its own: at several schools the published median sits lower than the school's published band shares would produce — the likeliest explanation is that the two statistics are counted over slightly different student sets. We print both as published and never "correct" a school's numbers.

Three schools set aside. St Joseph's College, Gregory Terrace (Spring Hill), St Peters Lutheran College's Springfield campus and Mt Alvernia College (Kedron) all publish medians above 90 alongside band shares showing fewer than half their students at 90 or above — arithmetically, both cannot describe the same cohort, so one figure is wrong or differently based and we will not rank a school on a number that cannot stand. Their published band shares (42.33%, 48.39% and 46.03% of students at 90+) are recorded here; if the schools clarify their bases, the rows return. Strong schools not ranked, because they publish no median: Sunshine Coast Grammar School, whose own annual report counts 48 per cent of all its Year 12 students at 90+ — a stricter basis than most — and The Rockhampton Grammar School (37.63 per cent at 90+ in its own release) would both sit in this table's top half on band strength; the Queensland Academy for Health Sciences (IB; 45.46 per cent at 95+) and Corinda State High School (9 students at 99+ and 31 at 95+ from a cohort of 349, published as counts) make the same point from the state sector. Absence from this table means "does not publish a median" — never "performed poorly". Three more names need the same honesty in reverse: St Andrew's Anglican College (Peregian Springs) has three conflicting medians in circulation and is excluded until its own figures can be read cleanly; The Glennie School's one striking figure exists in a single publisher's table and nowhere on the school's own site, so it is not used; and The Southport School published no 2025 figures that we could find.

A closer look at the top five

Brisbane Girls Grammar School — Spring Hill, Brisbane

The state's highest published median — 96.45 — comes with the state's most transparent paperwork. Brisbane Girls Grammar's own enrolment prospectus states that 100 per cent of its Year 12 cohort was ATAR-eligible and that its published figures rest on the 93.57 per cent of students who consented to share their ATAR with the school. Within that basis: 84.55 per cent of students scored 90 or above, 60.94 per cent scored 95 or above, and four students scored the maximum 99.95. A median of 96.45 means the middle student in this cohort outperformed roughly nineteen of every twenty students in the state age cohort — the strongest whole-cohort result published in Queensland for 2025.

Whitsunday Anglican School — Beaconsfield, Mackay

The finding of this table. Whitsunday Anglican — a co-educational independent K–12 school in Mackay, some 970 kilometres from Brisbane — published a median ATAR of 96.00, which its own results page calls the highest in the school's history, with a dux of 99.9 and the next student at 99.85. It also reported the highest share of students at 99+ in the state (28 per cent). Two cautions belong beside the celebration: it is a small regional cohort (the school does not publish its ATAR cohort size), and small medians swing year to year in a way Brisbane Grammar's cannot. But a regional school at number two in Queensland is a genuine result, not a data quirk — its median is confirmed on its own site.

Brisbane Grammar School — Spring Hill, Brisbane

Brisbane Grammar's own results reporting leads with depth at the very top: eight students scored 99.95 — the most of any school in Queensland — with 17 per cent of the cohort at 99+, half at 95+ and 69 per cent at 90+. Its published median of 95.00 (independently carried by two publisher tables, with band shares that agree with it) makes it the strongest boys' school result in the state, and the Spring Hill pair — Brisbane Girls Grammar and Brisbane Grammar, side by side at numbers one and three — the strongest school precinct in Queensland.

St Aidan's Anglican Girls' School — Corinda, Brisbane

St Aidan's is the top five's quiet argument against assuming these results require selective entry. From the school's own enrolment pages: it is a Prep to Year 12 day school for girls at 11 Ruthven Street, Corinda, founded in 1929, with roughly 1,200 students — and applications are accepted from birth and offered by date of receipt, with no entrance examination for domestic entry. Its published median is 94.95, on a basis (recorded by School Insights from the school's own publication) of 95 per cent of the cohort ATAR-eligible and 94 per cent of those consenting. An open-entry school whose middle student sits at 94.95 is doing something worth visiting to understand.

Queensland Academy for Science, Mathematics and Technology (QASMT) — Toowong, Brisbane

QASMT's 94.75 carries two flags no other top-five row needs: it is a government selective-entry school (admission through the Queensland Academies process, with entrance testing), and it teaches the IB Diploma exclusively — there is no QCE pathway — so its "ATAR" is QTAC's conversion of IB scores, not a QCE median. Its own school profile records a 2025 IB mean of 37.5, and the school operates in partnership with the University of Queensland at 78 Bywong Street, Toowong. We rank it where its converted figure falls — as Better Education does — with the flags printed rather than buried: compare it with the other Academies and with IB programmes, not head-to-head with QCE schools.

What the table tells you

Fee-paying schools dominate — the state sector's three entries each carry an asterisk of a different kind. Forty-seven of the 50 are independent (28) or Catholic (19) schools. The three government schools are QASMT (selective, IB-only, #5), Brisbane State High (#6 — itself partially selective, and holder of one of the state's 99.95s) and The Gap State High School (#29), an ordinary zoned school whose 86.80 is in some ways the most instructive figure in the table: it is what a strong comprehensive cohort looks like without selection or fees. Beyond them, the state sector's strength hides where a median table cannot see it — Corinda State High's nine students at 99+ never appear here because the school publishes counts, not a median.

The map is wider than Brisbane. Most of the table sits in the south-east corner, but number two is in Mackay, Townsville Grammar holds #15, St Luke's represents Bundaberg at #33, and the Gold Coast, Sunshine Coast, Ipswich and Logan all place schools in the top 30. Girls' schools take 15 places (including numbers one and four), boys' schools 13 and co-educational schools 22. And the whole table is compressed: from 96.45 at the top to 77.55 at number 50 — with the statewide recipient median near 80, the difference between a top-10 school and a top-50 school is the difference between an exceptional middle student and a merely strong one, not between success and failure.

Using this ranking well, step by step

  1. Shortlist on fit first — sector, faith, single-sex or co-ed, and where you actually live; in a table this compressed, a five-point median gap matters less than a daily two-hour commute.
  2. Ask each school for its basis: what share of the cohort was ATAR-eligible, and what share consented to share results? A median over 94 per cent of a fully eligible cohort (St Aidan's, Brisbane Girls Grammar) is a stronger claim than the same number on an unstated basis.
  3. Read the bands beside the median: the % at 90+ and 95+ shows whether a school's strength is a broad lift or a brilliant top tail — and check whether the two figures can describe the same cohort.
  4. For selective and IB routes (QASMT and the other Queensland Academies), treat entry as its own project — testing happens years ahead — and compare IB results with IB results, not with QCE medians.
  5. Re-check after December: the Class of 2026's results arrive on 16–18 December 2026, and Queensland medians — especially at small schools — can move several points in a year.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which Queensland school has the highest median ATAR for 2025?

Brisbane Girls Grammar School, with a school-published median ATAR of 96.45 for its Class of 2025 — calculated, as the school itself discloses, over the 93.57 per cent of its fully ATAR-eligible cohort who consented to share their ATAR with the school. Whitsunday Anglican School in Mackay is second at 96.00. Remember that Queensland publishes no official school league table: these are the schools' own figures, gathered and cross-checked as described in this page's methodology note.

Is there an official Queensland school league table?

No. Queensland's education authorities have released no school-by-school Year 12 results since 2020, and a school can only access a student's ATAR if the student consents to share it. Every Queensland ranking — this one, the newspaper tables and the tutoring-company lists alike — is therefore assembled from figures schools choose to calculate and publish about themselves. This page handles that honestly: a school's own current figure wins over any publisher's table, single-source figures are marked †, schools whose published median and band shares contradict each other are set aside rather than ranked, and strong schools that publish no median are named in the methodology note instead of silently dropped.

What does a median ATAR actually tell you?

The ATAR is a rank from 0.00 to 99.95 against the whole Year 12 age cohort — 99.95 is the top rank (37 Queensland students in 2025), and 90+ means roughly the top tenth of the age group. A school's median ATAR is the middle result among its own ATAR students: half did better, half worse. It is the most robust single figure a school publishes, because a few brilliant students cannot lift it the way they lift a "% at 99+" headline. Context matters, though: only about 52 per cent of Queensland students receive an ATAR at all, and QTAC puts the statewide median among recipients close to 80.

Why are some well-known Queensland schools missing from the top 50?

Almost always because they do not publish a median ATAR — not because their results are weak. Sunshine Coast Grammar School publishes band shares only (48 per cent of all its Year 12 students at 90+, a stricter basis than most schools use) and The Rockhampton Grammar School likewise (37.63 per cent at 90+); the Queensland Academy for Health Sciences and Corinda State High School publish bands or counts rather than medians. One school, St Andrew's Anglican College, is excluded because three conflicting medians for it are in circulation and none can be verified against its own band counts. In Queensland, absence from a ranking means "does not publish the ranked figure".

Does a higher median ATAR mean a school is better for my child?

Not on its own — and in Queensland, less than anywhere else in Australia. These medians are self-published, counted over differently defined cohorts, and one or two points apart at schools with entirely different intakes: selective academies examine their students in, open-entry schools like St Aidan's take them by application date, and small regional cohorts swing year to year. The table also says nothing about teaching in the middle bands, wellbeing or fit. Use it to build a shortlist and to ask sharper questions — each school's ATAR-eligible share and consent rate among them — then visit.
